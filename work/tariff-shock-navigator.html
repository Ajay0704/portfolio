The Challenge

Between January 2025 and April 2026, US tariffs on imports from China, Vietnam, India and Mexico changed again and again: new duties, rates announced and then suspended, exemptions added. A company that imports from these countries has to know what it pays today, product by product, and how much of that is new.

The answer is public, but scattered: import values sit with the Census Bureau, and the rates sit in the official tariff schedule as thousands of product lists across chapter 99. I wanted to see whether one analyst with a reproducible pipeline could put the two together for every product line.

What I Did

Import data. 2023 US imports at the most detailed product level (HS10) for the four countries, from the Census Bureau International Trade API: 42,327 product lines worth $1,098.1 billion.

Rates from the official schedule. I downloaded the US tariff schedule (USITC Harmonized Tariff Schedule) as it stood in April 2026, Revision 5, and extracted every product list that sets a duty: the normal (MFN) rate, Section 301 lists on China, Section 232 duties on metals, vehicles, trucks and wood, the 10% fentanyl duty on China, and the reciprocal tariffs (China 10%, Vietnam 20%, India 25%) with their exemption lists. Each line gets each layer separately, with the schedule note it came from.

Before and after. The same lines priced at the January 1, 2025 rates (MFN, Section 301 and the 2018 steel and aluminum duties) show how much of today’s bill is new.

Risk score from the data. Each line scores 0–10: half for how high its April 2026 rate is, half for how much it rose since January 2025. The equal weighting is my design choice; the inputs are computed.

Prices and reporting. A BLS import-price module flags month-over-month moves above 0.5% (2020–2025), eight DuckDB SQL queries summarise the results, and a script writes the executive briefing and six charts from the processed data.

Key Outcomes

  • At April 2026 rates, 2023’s imports from the four countries carry $214.4 billion a year in tariffs (19.5%), against $58.4 billion (5.3%) at January 2025 rates.
  • China carries 64% of the total ($137.8B): Section 301, the 10% fentanyl duty and the 10% reciprocal rate stack on most goods. Smartphones and laptops ($80B) pay 10% because they are exempt from the reciprocal tariff.
  • Vietnam and India went from 4.0% and 2.5% to about 20%, about four-fifths of it from the reciprocal tariff.
  • Mexico is not tariff-free. Even if every good claims USMCA, vehicles, trucks and metals carry $37.8B in Section 232 duties.
  • A reproducible pipeline: Census, USITC and BLS data in; rates, SQL summaries, charts and a briefing out, with a verification script that checks the totals and nine hand-calculated products.

Statutory estimate on a fixed 2023 import mix. Not modelled: supplier switching, per-unit duties, product exclusions, the US-content deduction on vehicles, and end-use-based semiconductor duties. Sources: US Census Bureau; USITC HTS 2026 Revision 5, chapter 99; BLS. Details in the repository README.

The Audit

The first version of this project reported $186.6 billion. When I audited it against the official sources, the import data and arithmetic held up, but the tariff rates did not: they were typed in by hand. Vietnam’s 46% and India’s 26% were the rates announced on April 2, 2025, which were suspended a week later and replaced in August 2025. Goods the orders exempt were still charged, normal duties were missing, the country risk scores were set by hand, and a date-parsing bug ran the price data backwards within each year.

I rebuilt the rates from the tariff schedule, product by product, replaced the hand-set scores with a computed one, and fixed the bug (the price spikes go from 125 to 114). The full list of changes is in the repository’s AUDIT.md.
