The Challenge

Every SaaS company in 2026 is asking the same two questions: is per-seat pricing dying, and how should we charge for AI? The industry answers with confident statistics — for example, an ICONIQ Capital survey of roughly 300 AI-building software companies, published by BCG, found 68% of vendors charge separately for AI or restrict it to premium tiers.

The trouble is the credible estimates measure different things and don’t agree — Kyle Poyar’s 230-company survey finds 29% use AI credits, a narrower measure, while the ICONIQ/BCG figure is 68% — and the highest numbers come from aggregator pages with no stated method. There was no open dataset to settle it.

So I built one: I collected pricing data from 198 leading SaaS companies directly from their live pricing pages, and used it to answer the questions with evidence instead of citation.

What I Did

Sampling. 198 recognisable B2B SaaS companies, 18 in each of 11 categories — dev tools, data & analytics, CRM, martech, collaboration, design, security, HR, fintech-ops, support and AI-native — equal numbers per category, so no single segment dominates the totals.

Collection. Each live pricing page was read and converted into 19 structured fields (26 columns in the public dataset): pricing model, tier names, entry price, how AI is charged, where SSO sits. Prices normalised to USD per month. No third-party datasets, no synthetic data.

Recovery. The most valuable pages fight back — Salesforce, OpenAI, Adobe and Canva block scrapers or render prices in JavaScript. Those were recovered with a headless browser, and 14 hard-blocked pages were collected by hand, lifting coverage from 80% to 95%.

Validation. Every headline count is rebuilt three ways — from the raw extraction files with separate code, by the analysis pipeline, and from the published CSV — and all agree. That checks the arithmetic; coding accuracy was measured separately (see The Audit). Every row in the public CSV carries its source URL and the quoted page text it was coded from.

How Visible Pricing Compares With the Surveys

Public pricing pages show somewhat less AI monetisation than the most-cited survey — 57% against 68% — while on AI credits specifically the pages and the survey agree. I am not claiming the reports are wrong: the sources measure different things, part of the higher figure is not visible on public pricing pages, and the gap comes from different definitions, populations and methods.

57% vs 68%

Like for like, public pricing pages show about the same share using AI credits as Poyar’s survey (27% vs 29%), and somewhat fewer charging extra in any form than ICONIQ reports (57% vs 68%). Both surveys are self-reported and sample different companies. Part of the gap is likely AI sold in enterprise contracts that never appears on a public page, so this measures visible pricing only.

The Audit

After publishing a first version I had it reviewed from a research-methods and an investor perspective, then tested my own data. A blinded recheck of 30 random rows against archived copies of the August pages agreed with the dataset on 86–100% of pricing-model, price and SSO codes, but only 71% on whether AI costs extra — and every disagreement pointed the same way: my first pass had coded AI as “bundled” when the page sold an add-on or let you buy more credits.

So I re-coded all 94 AI rows coded bundled or unclear, blind, from the archived pages. That moved the AI headline from 42% to 57% (95% range 49–65%; the strictest reading gives 46%, including live pages gives 64%). Raw extractions were never edited: all 137 field corrections are logged with the value replaced, the reason and the source.

Key Outcomes

  • Self-collected a dataset of 188 SaaS companies × 26 columns, every value traced to a pricing page — 95% coverage of the sampling frame.
  • Found that 57% of AI-offering companies visibly charge extra for AI on their pricing pages, below the 68% reported in self-reported surveys, and that on AI credits pages and surveys agree (27% vs 29%).
  • Audited my own headline: a blinded recheck showed the first AI figure (42%) under-counted add-ons and credit top-ups, so I re-coded all 94 affected rows from archived pages and corrected it publicly.
  • Quantified the “SSO tax” (69% of pages that list SSO gate it to the top tier) and built benchmarks that did not previously exist publicly (median 4 tiers; $14 median entry price; 20% median annual discount among the 85 companies that state one).
  • Built an interactive dashboard so anyone can filter the data by category and stage and check the findings themselves.
  • Documented limitations openly, including sample bias and small category sample sizes.
  • Published the full dataset for anyone to check: download the 188-company CSV ↓
