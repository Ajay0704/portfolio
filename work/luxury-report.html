Luxury’s lost growth

Where the industry’s growth went between 2023 and 2026, which houses kept growing, and what each type of house should do next.

Built from the annual and interim reports of 12 luxury houses (2,252 figures, each traced to its filing), the Bain–Altagamma, BCG–Altagamma, McKinsey–BoF and Deloitte studies, official statistics and daily share prices. Every number on this page links to its source in the appendix.

Executive summary
  1. The market stalled; it didn’t crash. Personal luxury goods sales slipped from €369bn in 2023 to €358bn in 2025, and Bain expects about €367bn in 2026. The real change is underneath: the number of buyers fell from about 400 million in 2022 to about 315 million, and the biggest spenders now make up 48% of sales, up from 30% in 2019.
  2. The boom was bought with price. Average luxury prices rose about 61% from 2019 to 2025, and about 80% of 2023–2025 growth came from price rather than more items sold (McKinsey–BoF). The US list price of a Chanel Classic 11.12 bag went from $5,600 to $11,700 (+109%), against +29% for US consumer prices. Now about 70% of luxury shoppers say they walked away from a purchase in the past year because the price felt unjustified (BCG).
  3. The slump is concentrated in a few large businesses. Across the 11 houses we could compare, operating profit fell €8.1bn (−17%) from 2023 to 2025. LVMH’s Fashion & Leather Goods division (−€3.6bn) and Gucci (−€2.3bn) account for 74% of the fall. Six houses grew profit over the same period: Hermès, Tapestry, Ralph Lauren, Prada, Brunello Cucinelli and Moncler.
  4. Demand moved; it didn’t disappear. Jewellery is the fastest-growing category in 2026 (+8–10%, Bain). Richemont’s jewellery houses raised operating profit to €5.0bn while its watchmakers’ profit fell 81%. Bain expects the Americas to grow fastest in 2026 (+7–9%). Even China is split: in Jul–Sep 2026 Tapestry grew 39% in Greater China and Ralph Lauren 28% in Asia, while LVMH and Kering each fell 5% in Asia.
  5. 2026 added outside shocks to a weakened model. The war that began on 28 February cut Dubai’s visitors by 44% (Jan–Aug). China tightened tax on wealthy residents’ offshore income and trusts. Chinese visits to Japan more than halved after a travel advisory. US luxury card spending fell 6% in September (Citi). Gold, a jewellery input, averaged $3,431 an ounce in 2025, up from $1,941 in 2023. In September both the Fed and the ECB raised rates.
  6. Investors first marked down earnings, then the price they pay for them. From end-2023 to end-2025 LVMH’s operating profit fell 22% and its shares 12%. In 2026 its shares fell another 40% on organic growth of +2%. Hermès fell 40% while growing 5.5%.
  7. Eight moves would rebuild growth; their priority depends on the type of house. For the big fashion houses the priorities are fixing price against perceived value, reopening an entry point, and restarting new product. For jewellers, they are protecting margins against gold and building resale. For houses where scarcity is the product, they are deepening relationships with existing clients without diluting that scarcity. Accessible houses need to keep the brand momentum that let them raise prices. All of them need to plan for AI shopping assistants, where 70% of luxury questions name no brand (Bain).

Chapter 1

The market stalled and lost buyers

After the post-COVID boom, the personal luxury goods market peaked at €369bn in 2023 and has moved sideways since. Bain–Altagamma’s November 2026 estimate is about €367bn for 2026, +1–3% at current exchange rates. The headline hides a sharper change: the number of people buying luxury goods has fallen by about 85 million since 2022.

So what. With fewer buyers, growth depends on a narrower group of very wealthy clients. Bain puts top customers at 48% of personal luxury spending in 2026, up from 30% in 2019. BCG finds that clients spending more than €50,000 a year are 0.2% of buyers but 24% of spending. That concentration makes houses more profitable per client and more fragile: when a few wealthy groups pull back at once, as in 2026, nothing cushions the fall.

Chapter 2

Growth was bought with price

Three independent sources point the same way. McKinsey–BoF estimate that average luxury prices rose about 61% from 2019 to 2025 and that about 80% of 2023–2025 growth came from price. Bain finds like-for-like prices of iconic bags up 50–70% over 2019–2025, while launches of new “hero” bags fell 70–80% compared with 2016–2019. Our own check of archived US list prices gives a more mixed picture. Chanel’s Classic 11.12 bag more than doubled (+12% a year against about 4% inflation), and Louis Vuitton’s Neverfull rose 70%. But the Speedy, the Lady Dior and Coach’s Tabby rose only a little faster than inflation, and Cartier’s Love bracelet rose more slowly, up to mid-2025. Increases varied widely by product. The steepest were on Chanel’s Classic bag, Prada’s Re-Edition and the Neverfull. Much of the industry-wide price effect probably also came from selling a richer mix of more expensive products, which list prices cannot show (our inference).

So what. Price was not the mistake on its own. Coach (handbag average selling price up mid-teens in its year to Jun 2026) and Ralph Lauren (average unit retail up mid-teens in its year to Mar 2026, +9% in Jul–Sep 2026) raised prices and still added millions of new customers. Coach’s Tabby list price roughly kept pace with inflation, so its higher average price came mostly from less discounting and a richer product mix (our inference). The difference is where they started and what came with the price. They began from accessible prices and paired each increase with more desirable product. The big fashion houses pushed prices on unchanged icons to levels that took entry buyers out of the market. BCG’s survey shows the cost: about 70% of consumers, top clients included, walked away from a purchase because the price felt unjustified, and about 35% of aspirational buyers cut back or paused spending in 2025.

Chapter 3

A two-speed industry

Average figures hide a wide spread. Some houses grew by double digits through 2024 and 2025, while others shrank by a fifth. The damage to profit sits in a handful of businesses.

So what. LVMH’s Fashion & Leather Goods division and Gucci together lost €5.9bn of operating profit, almost three-quarters of the industry’s net decline. Meanwhile Coach added €0.7bn and Richemont’s jewellery houses €0.3bn. This is a fall concentrated in particular businesses, and the fixes must be just as specific.

Chapter 4

Where demand moved

The money that stopped going to bags and clothes went to three places. It went to jewellery. It went to the Americas and to local shoppers in Japan. And it went to brands that offered something new at a price people accepted, including in China.

So what. China did not simply stop buying. In Jul–Sep 2026 Tapestry grew 39% in Greater China, Ralph Lauren 28% in Asia and, in Apr–Sep, Burberry 10% in Greater China, while LVMH and Kering fell 5% in Asia. Bain estimates mainland China at −1 to −3% for 2026. Chinese shoppers are still spending, but they are choosing value, novelty and jewellery over the most expensive leather goods. The Americas are the main growth engine (+7–9% in 2026, Bain), though Bain notes that about three-quarters of first-half growth there came from price.

Chapter 5

The 2026 shocks

2026 brought outside shocks on several fronts at once. None would have broken a healthy industry on its own. Together they hit the same wealthy and travelling clients the big houses had come to rely on.

Chapter 6

What investors priced

Share prices tell a two-part story. In 2024 and 2025 investors marked most stocks down, though by less than profits fell, betting on a recovery. In 2026 they cut the price they pay for those profits, even at houses that are still growing.

So what. The 2026 sell-off is investors questioning the industry’s model, not a judgement on one quarter. LVMH and Hermès each lost about 40% while still reporting growth. We also tested whether the 2026 fall tracked AI stocks, as some coverage suggested. Once the overall market is accounted for, it did not: luxury fell on its own problems. For management teams this matters, because investors will reward evidence of volume growth and new clients, not another price increase.

Chapter 7

Five types of house, five different problems

The useful unit of analysis is the business model, not the company. LVMH alone runs a mega-brand fashion business, a jeweller and a beauty retailer. Grouping by how a house makes its money explains the 2024–2026 results better than size or nationality.

Chapter 8

Eight moves to win growth back

These are our recommendations, so they are judgement. Each rests on the evidence above, names a house where something similar worked, and sizes the prize with stated assumptions. The sizes are illustrative ranges, not forecasts.

1. Re-earn the price, and reopen the entry door

Evidence: Medium
Who should lead: Mega-brands first; creative-cycle brands
WhyAbout 70% of luxury consumers walked away from a purchase because the price felt unjustified (BCG, 2026). Buyers fell by about 85 million from 2022 to 2026 (Bain). Yet more than 75% of aspirational buyers expect to keep or raise their spending (BCG), and McKinsey sizes the underserved “established and aspirational” client pool at US$70–90bn.
What to doHold or selectively lower list prices on the core icons where the gap to perceived value is widest, and measure units sold, not just revenue. Build a real entry range, around €1,000–3,000, of well-made leather goods, small accessories and fragrance-led gifting that carries the house’s craft rather than a bigger logo. Track first-time buyers and second purchases as a board-level KPI.
Proof it worksCoach: revenue +23% at constant currency in the year to Jun 2026, a segment margin of 35.8%, and 11.0m new customers across Tapestry, with its Tabby priced at about the rate of inflation.
Size of the prize (illustrative)If LVMH Fashion & Leather Goods won back the 6% of organic sales it lost in 2024–2025, that would be about €2.4bn of revenue, or €0.8–1.2bn of operating profit at the division’s 35% margin or a 50% incremental margin. For Chanel, each 1% of volume is about $193m of revenue.
Measure it byUnits per store; new-to-brand clients; share of sales under €3,000; second-purchase rate within 12 months.
Watch out forCheapening the brand. Any entry range must be built on craft, not logos, and kept separate from the icons.

2. Grow where the category is growing

Evidence: High
Who should lead: Mega-brands with jewellery businesses; jewellers
WhyBain expects jewellery to grow 8–10% in 2026, against 0–2% for leather goods and −3 to −5% for shoes. Richemont’s Jewellery Maisons lifted operating profit from €4.7bn to €5.0bn while its watchmakers fell from €572m to €107m. Kering Jewelry grew 17% in Jan–Sep 2026.
What to doPut capital and store space behind jewellery and other high-value hard luxury. Build signature collections that become icons (the Love and Alhambra model). Hedge gold costs and price with care, given that China’s gold and jewellery retail sales fell 28.7% in October 2026.
Proof it worksRichemont: Jewellery Maisons sales +19% at constant rates in Apr–Sep 2026, with a 33.4% operating margin excluding tariff one-offs.
Size of the prize (illustrative)Jewellery is a €32bn market (2025). At 8–10% growth that is €2.6–3.2bn of new spending in 2026 alone. LVMH Watches & Jewelry earned a 14.4% margin in 2025 against Richemont’s 30.5%; each margin point on its €10.5bn of sales is worth about €105m.
Measure it byJewellery share of sales; jewellery gross margin after gold hedging; repeat purchase of signature lines.
Watch out forGold prices (spot peak $5,589/oz on 28 Jan 2026, per Kitco, and below $3,900 by mid-November, per Reuters). A weaker US jewellery shopper. Watches only began to recover in Apr–Sep 2026 (Richemont’s watchmakers +10%).

3. Restart the product engine

Evidence: Medium
Who should lead: Mega-brands; creative-cycle brands
WhyBain finds launches of new hero bags fell 70–80% in 2023–2025 compared with 2016–2019, while prices of the old ones rose. When Gucci’s second-quarter sales beat expectations, Kering’s shares rose 16.9% on the day (29 Jul 2026, WWD). BCG finds 68% of top clients know the creative director, against 44% of all consumers.
What to doFund a pipeline of genuinely new product families every season, alongside the icons. Give creative directors time: Kering changed the creative lead at three houses in October–November 2026. Measure the share of sales from products launched in the last 24 months.
Proof it worksMiu Miu (retail +58% in 2023 and +93% in 2024) and Coach’s Tabby show how far new product can carry a house. Miu Miu’s stall to +0.8% in 2026 shows it has to be renewed.
Size of the prize (illustrative)Gucci’s revenue fell from €9.9bn (2023) to €6.0bn (2025). Winning back a third of the €3.9bn lost, at a 30–40% incremental margin, would add €0.4–0.5bn of operating profit.
Measure it bySales from products launched in the last 24 months; full-price sell-through; launch hit rate.
Watch out forNewness that dilutes the house codes, and frequent creative changes that confuse clients.

4. Rebalance exposure across regions and channels

Evidence: High
Who should lead: All; most urgent for houses with high Gulf, tourist or department-store exposure
WhyTourists made up 40% of personal luxury spending in 2019 and 30–35% in 2026 (Bain). Shocks hit through travel corridors: Dubai visitors −44% (Jan–Aug 2026), Chinese arrivals in Japan −54.7% (Jan–Oct 2026). Saks Global’s bankruptcy left Chanel as the largest unsecured trade creditor at $136m. Meanwhile Bain sees the Americas growing 7–9% in 2026.
What to doBuild local client bases in each market, especially local Japanese, American and Korean clients, rather than following tourists. Set exposure limits on any single travel corridor or wholesale partner. Prefer concessions and own stores to credit-heavy wholesale.
Proof it worksRichemont: Japan +30% in Apr–Sep 2026 on double-digit growth in both local and tourist demand, even as Chinese arrivals fell. Burberry’s Americas comparable sales rose 12%, driven by local customers.
Size of the prize (illustrative)Mostly a risk reduction rather than new sales. Richemont’s Middle East and Africa region (9% of sales) swung from +22% to −7% in a year. Houses that limit any single corridor to a small share of sales cap that kind of swing.
Measure it byShare of sales to local clients by market; share of sales through the top three tourist corridors; wholesale credit exposure.
Watch out forBuilding local demand takes years. Pulling out of wholesale too fast cuts reach.

5. Own the second life of the product

Evidence: Medium
Who should lead: Jewellers and watchmakers first; all houses
WhySecondhand personal luxury reached about €50bn in 2025, growing 7–8% a year since 2019, three to four times faster than new goods (Bain). About half of shoppers check resale before buying new. 53.8% of luxury executives already run certified pre-owned or trade-in programmes (Deloitte, 2026).
What to doOffer certified pre-owned and trade-in credit through your own stores, so the trade-in becomes the next new purchase. Use authentication and repair to own the relationship over the product’s life. Treat resale prices as a live gauge of desirability.
Proof it works83% of secondhand luxury sales are hard luxury (Bain), which is why watch and jewellery houses have the most to gain. BCG and Vestiaire project watch resale at 35–40% of the watch market by 2030.
Size of the prize (illustrative)At 4–6% growth, the €50bn secondhand market adds €2–3bn of spending in 2026. A brand-run programme captures part of that and, more importantly, the next new purchase.
Measure it byTrade-in credit redeemed on new products; share of the brand’s own resale volume that goes through its channels.
Watch out forCannibalising new sales, and the cost of authentication.

6. Win the AI shelf

Evidence: Lower
Who should lead: All; urgent for brands that rely on discovery
WhyBain finds 70% of luxury questions put to AI assistants name no brand, and visitors referred by AI spend over 40% more per visit. About half of luxury consumers already use AI somewhere in the purchase journey. BCG finds generative AI’s net trust score about twice that of social media.
What to doMake product data, availability, materials and provenance clean and readable by AI assistants. Track how often the house is named in answers to unbranded questions (“best first designer bag”, “bracelet that holds its value”). Train client advisers to work alongside AI-led shoppers.
Proof it worksBain’s 2026 finding that AI-referred visitors spend over 40% more per visit. Brands are starting: LVMH’s “AI for ALL” plan focuses on staff and client advisers.
Size of the prize (illustrative)Not yet measurable from public data. The prize is a share of the 70% of AI questions that don’t name a brand.
Measure it byShare of unbranded AI answers that name the house; AI-referred sessions and spend per visit.
Watch out forPlatforms change fast, and errors in product data are amplified.

7. Deepen the relationships that are still growing

Evidence: High
Who should lead: Scarcity houses; jewellers; mega-brands’ top-client teams
WhyTop customers spend about €780bn, more than half of all luxury spending (Bain, 2026). Older generations account for 45–55% of spending, and Bain estimates longer, healthier lives could add €300–600bn of cumulative value in 2026–2030. Experiences (€533bn) have grown faster than goods every year since 2023.
What to doInvest in client advisers, private appointments and service for top and long-standing clients across all their categories. Add experiences (hospitality, travel, cultural access) that deepen the relationship rather than one-off events. Design for older clients, not only Gen Z.
Proof it worksHermès: growth every year through the slowdown (+14.7% in 2024, +8.9% in 2025, +5.5% in Jan–Sep 2026) with a 41% margin, built on a clienteling model.
Size of the prize (illustrative)Top-client spending is the largest single pool in luxury. Bain’s longevity estimate alone is €300–600bn over five years across the industry.
Measure it byTop-client retention; share of wallet across categories; spend per adviser.
Watch out forOver-concentration: the 2026 shocks showed the danger of depending on a few wealthy groups.

8. Make every store and every euro earn its place

Evidence: Medium
Who should lead: Mega-brands; creative-cycle brands
WhyBain puts the industry’s operating margin at 15–16% in 2025, back to 2009 levels. LVMH’s store count rose from 6,097 (2023) to 6,283 (2025) while revenue fell, so revenue per store dropped from €14.1m to €12.9m (−9%). Kering’s store count fell from 1,794 (2024) to 1,614 (Sep 2026), and it plans at least 125 closures in 2026.
What to doClose or resize low-productivity stores, especially in tourist locations that no longer draw shoppers. Shift spending from opening stores to product and clients. Tie marketing spend to new-client growth.
Proof it worksBurberry: adjusted operating margin back from 1.0% (FY to Mar 2025) to 6.6% (FY to Mar 2026) after its reset, with Apr–Sep 2026 sales +6%.
Size of the prize (illustrative)Each point of operating margin on LVMH’s 2025 revenue is worth about €0.81bn.
Measure it bySales per square metre; store four-wall margin; marketing spend per new client.
Watch out forCutting too far into the brand experience that justifies the price.

Chapter 10

Method, limits and sources

How the fact base was built

  • Company figures. 2,252 figures from the annual reports, results releases and SEC filings of 12 houses, each with its document and page or table. An automated check confirmed each figure appears in its cited source. Segment and regional sums were checked against group totals.
  • Calendar matching. A fiscal year counts toward the calendar year that holds most of its months. For June year-ends, the earlier year.
  • Growth. Each company’s own organic, comparable or constant-currency measure, kept separate and labelled.
  • Profit pool. Operating profit for 2023 and 2025, recurring or adjusted where the company reports it and reported otherwise (Richemont, Chanel). Kering’s 2023 figure includes Kering Beauté, which it later sold. On a recurring basis for Richemont the total would be about −€7.9bn. US dollar and sterling figures converted at 2025 average rates. Capri is excluded because the Versace sale changes its scope.
  • Icon prices. US list prices read from brand product pages saved by the Internet Archive, one reading per year. A script confirmed each price appears on the saved page.
  • Share prices. Yahoo Finance daily closes to 18 Nov 2026.

What this can’t show

  • Most houses don’t report units, so price and volume can only be separated indirectly.
  • List prices of a few icons aren’t the average price paid. Mix, discounts and regional prices all differ.
  • Bain, BCG and McKinsey figures are estimates. Some McKinsey figures reached us through press coverage, and Bain’s full 2026 report isn’t public yet.
  • Definitions differ: Bain counts about 315m personal luxury buyers, BCG 429m “true-luxury” consumers.
  • Macro links (tax, war, travel) are the companies’ and analysts’ explanations, matched to data; we can’t prove cause.
  • The sizes in Chapter 8 are illustrative ranges built from stated assumptions, not forecasts.
  • This is business analysis, not investment advice.
